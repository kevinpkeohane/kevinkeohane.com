What I solve

Five problems I've spent 20 years fixing

Each one sounds like a marketing problem. None of them are.

01 / 05
We spend on marketing and I can't see the return.
Proof · 40+ GTM playbooks for Deloitte, $10M–$10B+ targets

It's rarely the marketing. The positioning was never actually settled, so sales improvises a different story in every room and the agency optimises hard against the wrong message. The expensive part isn't the wasted budget. It's eighteen months spent solving the wrong problem competently.

02 / 05
We're better than them. On paper we look the same.
Proof · EarthScope — $400M+ contract won and defended

Categories reach parity on the page long before they reach it in practice. A comparison grid rewards whatever fits inside a cell, so the better offer scores the same as the weaker one. You don't win by arguing harder inside the cell. You win by changing what the buyer believes the cells should be — before the criteria get written.

03 / 05
We agreed the strategy. Then nothing happened.
Proof · EY — one story, 150 countries, 300,000 people

It was written centrally, admired in the room, and quietly ignored by the people expected to carry it. Adoption is the deliverable, not the deck. A strategy that needs authority to travel doesn't travel — it has to be built for the people who will use it, not the people who will approve it.

04 / 05
We're promising what our own people can't deliver.
Proof · EX2CX methodology · two books on where brand and talent converge

The promise outruns operational reality and the company pays for the gap twice — in attrition and in churn. By the time it surfaces in Glassdoor and CX scores it has been costing money for two years. EX2CX maps employee-experience friction to customer-experience failure: culture priced as a commercial problem, not an HR one.

05 / 05
We've become several companies sharing a logo.
Proof · Dormakaba · LTIMindtree — five AI offerings, one idea

Multiple products, segments or acquisitions, each arriving with its own story — then flattened into sameness or fragmented into incoherence. Neither is a messaging problem. It's architecture: what holds at the top, what flexes underneath, and who decides.
